Most advice about social media is written for brands that want to reach the whole country, or the whole world. A business in Blessington has a different job. You don't need a huge following. You need the right people in and around the town to know who you are, trust you, and think of you first when they need what you offer.

That changes how you should approach it.

## Relevance beats reach

A follower on the other side of the world who will never visit your shop is worth very little to you. A family a few streets away who sees your post, recognises your face and books you next week is worth a great deal.

Blessington had 5,611 residents at the 2022 census, and new housing developments have brought more families since. That is a real, reachable audience. The goal isn't to be big online. It is to be relevant to the people who live within a short drive of your door.

Relevance comes from being local, useful and recognisable. Follower counts are nice to look at, but they don't pay the bills. Enquiries from nearby customers do.

## Where local customers actually are

You don't need to be everywhere. You need to be where your customers already spend time.

**Community Facebook groups.** In many Irish towns, the local Facebook groups are where people ask for recommendations, share news and find services. When someone posts "Can anyone recommend a good electrician?", those threads matter. Join the groups that serve Blessington and the surrounding areas, follow their rules on promotion, and be genuinely helpful rather than posting ads. Answering a question well does more for you than a sales post.

**Instagram.** This is where people go to see what a business looks and feels like. It works especially well for anything visual: food, beauty, fitness, interiors, trades with before-and-after work. Your grid is often the first thing someone checks after hearing your name.

**LinkedIn, if you sell to other businesses.** If your customers are companies rather than households, LinkedIn is where decision-makers will check you out. A clear profile, a company page and occasional posts about real work are usually enough to make you look established.

Pick one or two of these and do them properly. A business that shows up well on one platform beats a business that shows up badly on four.

## Content that works locally

The best local content isn't polished advertising. It is real.

**Real faces.** People buy from people. Show who runs the business, who answers the phone, who turns up at the door. A friendly face makes a new customer far more comfortable getting in touch.

**Behind the scenes.** Show how the work gets done: the prep before opening, the tools, the care that goes into a job. It builds trust because it proves the quality instead of just claiming it.

**Local places.** Mention the places people know. A job finished in Burgage, a delivery out towards Three Castles, a morning walk by the lakes before opening. When locals recognise the setting, they feel you are part of their community. Because you are.

**Real projects.** Before and after photos, finished work, a short clip of a happy customer, shared with their permission. One honest example of good work is more persuasive than any slogan.

None of this needs expensive equipment. A modern phone, good light and a steady hand will cover most of it. What matters is that it looks like you and shows what you actually do.

## Consistency beats volume

You will recognise this pattern. A business page that posts "Happy Christmas!" in December, "Happy Easter!" in spring, and very little in between. Seasonal greetings aren't wrong. But if that is all a page posts, it tells people nothing about the business, and it quietly suggests nobody is really there.

Social platforms reward accounts that show up regularly, and so do people. When you post steadily, your name stays in front of locals between the moments they need you. When you go quiet for months, they forget. The next time they need what you offer, they ask the group, and someone else gets mentioned.

You don't need to post every day. You need to post reliably, at a pace you can keep for a year, not a fortnight.

## A simple monthly rhythm

Here is a rhythm most small businesses can keep without it taking over the week. It works out at one post a week, plus a little time in the community.

**Week one: show the work.** A recent project, a finished job, a new product or dish. Photos or a short video, with a line or two on what you did and who it was for.

**Week two: show the people.** Introduce yourself or someone on the team. What they do, how long they've been doing it, something human about them.

**Week three: be useful.** Answer a question customers often ask you. A quick tip, a seasonal reminder, a common mistake to avoid. It positions you as the expert people can trust.

**Week four: show the place.** Something local. A job in a part of town people know, an event you supported, a nod to a neighbouring business you work with.

Alongside that, spend a few minutes each week in the local groups: answering questions, thanking customers who mention you, and replying to every comment and message promptly. That part is free, and it is often where the enquiries actually come from.

Batch it if you can. An hour at the start of the month to take photos and write captions saves you scrambling every week.

## Where social media leads

Social media gets people interested. It rarely closes the sale on its own. When someone likes what they see, they tap through to your profile, then to your website or Google listing, and decide there whether to get in touch. If that next step looks dated or confusing, the good work your posts did is lost.
